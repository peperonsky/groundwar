Three days in July 1863

For three days at the start of July 1863, the fields around this small Pennsylvania town held the largest battle ever fought in North America. Robert E. Lee had taken the Confederate Army of Northern Virginia north across the Potomac into Pennsylvania, hoping that a victory on Northern soil would weaken the Union’s will to keep fighting. George G. Meade’s Union Army of the Potomac followed, and the two armies collided at Gettysburg, where several roads met.

On 1 July, Confederate infantry ran into Union cavalry west of the town. The fighting grew through the day as both armies hurried more men toward the sound of the guns, until the Confederates drove the Union troops back through the streets to Cemetery Hill, just south of the town, where they dug in. By the second day the Union line ran along Cemetery Ridge in the shape of a fish hook, from Culp’s Hill and Cemetery Hill in the north to the two rocky hills called the Round Tops in the south.

On 2 July, Lee attacked both ends of that line. In the south the fighting swept through places whose names became famous: the Peach Orchard, the Wheatfield, Devil’s Den and Little Round Top. In the evening, Confederates attacked East Cemetery Hill and Culp’s Hill at the northern end. The Union line bent, but held.

On 3 July, after one of the heaviest artillery bombardments of the war, about 12,500 Confederate infantry marched nearly a mile across open fields toward the centre of the Union line on Cemetery Ridge. Pickett’s Charge, as it came to be called, was cut to pieces by artillery and rifle fire: more than half of the men who took part were killed, wounded or captured. On 4 July Lee began his retreat to Virginia. Some 23,000 Union soldiers and between 23,000 and 28,000 Confederates had been killed, wounded, captured or gone missing: the costliest battle of the Civil War.

The fighting around the town

The battle didn’t stay on the famous fields, and the list below shows how far it spread. Cavalry fought across the countryside before, during and after it. At Hanover, 22 km to the east, J.E.B. Stuart’s Confederate horsemen ran into Union cavalry on 30 June; the delay helped keep Stuart away from Lee until the second day of the battle. At Hunterstown, 9 km northeast, the two sides’ cavalry clashed on 2 July. At Fairfield, 12 km west, a Confederate victory on 3 July kept open the Hagerstown Road, the route Lee’s army took home. And at Monterey Pass, 23 km southwest, Union cavalry attacked the retreating Confederate wagon train on the night of 4 July, taking prisoners and destroying hundreds of wagons.

Why it mattered

Lee never invaded the North again. On 4 July, the day his retreat began, the Confederate garrison of Vicksburg on the Mississippi surrendered, and together the two defeats are often called the turning point of the war. Gettysburg’s own people were left with thousands of wounded in their houses, churches and barns. Only one of them died in the fighting: Jennie Wade, aged 20, killed by a stray bullet on 3 July.

Four and a half months later, on 19 November 1863, Abraham Lincoln came to dedicate the Soldiers’ National Cemetery, where the Union dead were being reburied, and gave the Gettysburg Address: fewer than three hundred words that became one of the best-known speeches in American history.

Visiting

Most of the battlefield is protected as Gettysburg National Military Park, run by the National Park Service: fields, ridges, farmhouses and hundreds of monuments, which you can follow by car, on foot or with a guide. The park’s Museum and Visitor Center holds the Gettysburg Cyclorama, an enormous 19th-century circular painting of Pickett’s Charge, and the National Cemetery where Lincoln spoke is at the edge of the town.
