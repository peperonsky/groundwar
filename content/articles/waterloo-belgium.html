The battle that wasn’t quite at Waterloo

The Battle of Waterloo, on Sunday 18 June 1815, was fought a few kilometres south of the town, across the farmland around Mont-Saint-Jean, the walled farms of Hougoumont and La Haye Sainte, and the village of Plancenoit. Waterloo was where the Duke of Wellington had his headquarters and wrote his report of the victory, and his name for it stuck. The French called it the Battle of Mont-Saint-Jean, and the Prussians La Belle Alliance, after an inn on the battlefield.

Napoleon’s last campaign

Early in 1815 Napoleon escaped from exile on the island of Elba, and by the end of March he was back in power in Paris. The rest of Europe declared war on him again, and two armies gathered in what is now Belgium: an Anglo-allied army of British, Dutch, Belgian and German troops under Wellington, and a Prussian army under Field Marshal Blücher. Napoleon’s plan was to strike before they could join forces and beat them one at a time.

On 16 June he fought both at once. He beat the Prussians at Ligny, while his left wing under Marshal Ney fought Wellington at Quatre Bras, 17 km south of Waterloo. Wellington held the crossroads, but couldn’t help the Prussians, and both allied armies fell back: Wellington to a ridge near Mont-Saint-Jean, the Prussians toward Wavre, 15 km east of Waterloo, promising to come to his aid.

18 June 1815

Heavy rain overnight turned the fields to mud and the French attack didn’t begin until late morning. All day, Wellington’s men held Hougoumont, the château and farm in front of his right wing, against attack after attack. In the afternoon, massed French cavalry charged again and again at the allied infantry, who formed squares to meet them. The farm of La Haye Sainte, in the centre of the line, fell to the French late in the day.

By then the Prussians were arriving from the east and fighting their way into Plancenoit, behind the French right. In the evening Napoleon sent forward his Imperial Guard. When it was driven back, the French army broke and fled. Some 25,000 French soldiers had been killed or wounded and 8,000 captured; Wellington’s army had lost about 17,000 men and the Prussians about 7,000. Meanwhile at Wavre, a Prussian rearguard held off 33,000 French troops under Marshal Grouchy for two days, keeping them away from the battle that decided the war.

Napoleon abdicated four days later and was exiled to Saint Helena, in the South Atlantic, where he died in 1821. Waterloo ended more than twenty years of war between France and the rest of Europe, and its name became a byword for a final, decisive defeat.

Around Waterloo

Waterloo is 15 km south of Brussels, so the list below reaches back long before 1815: Brussels was bombarded by the French in 1695, during the Nine Years’ War, and besieged in 1708 and again in 1746. In May 1940 the French army fought the German advance at Gembloux, 24 km to the southeast.

Visiting

The Lion’s Mound, an artificial hill built between 1820 and 1826 where the Prince of Orange is thought to have been wounded, gives a view over the whole battlefield, with the battlefield visitor centre at its foot. Hougoumont was restored and opened to the public for the battle’s bicentenary in 2015. In the town itself, the inn Wellington used as his headquarters is now a museum.
