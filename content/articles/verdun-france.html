302 days in 1916

The Battle of Verdun began on 21 February 1916 and ended on 18 December, 302 days later. It was one of the longest and costliest battles in history. When it was over, the front line was close to where it had started, more than 700,000 French and German soldiers had been killed, wounded or captured, and well over 200,000 of them were dead.

Verdun was a fortress city on the river Meuse, ringed by forts on the hills around it. In February 1916 the German army, under its chief of staff Erich von Falkenhayn, attacked from the north. By his own later account, the aim was less to break through than to force France to defend Verdun at any cost, and bleed its army white.

How the battle went

The attack opened with an enormous bombardment. In the Caures Woods, 13 km north of the city, two battalions of French light infantry under Lieutenant-Colonel Émile Driant held up the advance for two days; Driant was killed there. On 25 February, Fort Douaumont, the largest and highest of Verdun’s forts, was taken almost without a fight: it had been partly disarmed and left nearly unmanned the year before.

General Philippe Pétain was put in charge of the defence. With the main railway lines into Verdun cut or under fire, most of what the French army needed came up a single road from Bar-le-Duc, kept open by an endless line of lorries and later named the Voie Sacrée, the Sacred Way. French divisions were rotated through Verdun in turn, so that most of the French army fought there at some point.

Through the spring the fighting moved slowly across the hills, from the Mort-Homme on the west bank of the Meuse to Fort Vaux on the east, which fell in June. From July, Germany had to send men and guns to the Somme instead, and in the autumn French counter-attacks under Robert Nivelle and Charles Mangin took back Fort Douaumont, in October, and most of the ground lost since February.

What it left behind

The battlefield was shelled so heavily that several villages on it were wiped out and never rebuilt. Fleury-devant-Douaumont is one: officially it still exists, with a mayor, as a village that “died for France”. Much of the ground is still forest planted over the shell holes, part of the zone rouge that France set aside after the war as too damaged to live on. For France, Verdun became the symbol of the whole war.

Among the notable people below are some of those it touched. Franz Marc, the German Expressionist painter, was killed at Verdun in March 1916. The French novelist Alain-Fournier, author of Le Grand Meaulnes, was killed in the woods southeast of the city in September 1914, and Henry Gunther, an American soldier, was killed 17 km to the north at 10:59 on 11 November 1918, a minute before the armistice, possibly the last soldier of the war to die.

Before 1916, and after

Verdun’s name was already in the history books. The Treaty of Verdun of 843 divided Charlemagne’s empire between his three grandsons, roughly along the lines that later became France and Germany. In 1792, at the start of the French Revolutionary Wars, a Prussian army took the city on its way toward Paris, an advance that was stopped weeks later at Valmy. And in the autumn of 1918, the American army’s largest offensive of the war, the Meuse–Argonne, was fought just to the northwest.

Visiting

The Douaumont Ossuary holds the bones of at least 130,000 unidentified French and German soldiers, which can be seen through small windows along its base, and it looks out over a French national cemetery. Forts Douaumont and Vaux can both be visited, and the Verdun Memorial museum stands by the site of Fleury. The road from Bar-le-Duc is still called the Voie Sacrée.
