About Groundwar

Groundwar is a free map of what happened where you stand: the battles, sieges, treaties, revolutions and disasters around any place on Earth, and the notable people who were born or died there.

Open the map →

Why it exists

Most places have a past that never makes it onto a sign. A field outside town may be where an army was broken, and a quiet suburb may sit on the line of an old siege. Wikipedia has articles on tens of thousands of these events, but it files them by name, so you have to know what you're looking for before you can find it. Groundwar starts from the place instead: type a town, or use your location, and it shows everything recorded within the distance you choose, nearest first.

What's on it

  • More than 26,000 battles, sieges, military operations, treaties, revolutions and disasters, each pinned where it happened and dated where the date is known.
  • More than 330,000 notable people, shown where they were born and where they died.
  • 1,200 place pages, one for each town or city with a battlefield on its doorstep or a long record of battles and events within 25 km: what happened there, which wars it was part of, and who was born or died there.
  • On this day: the best-known battles and events that happened on today's date, and people born on it, on the homepage and the places page.

On the map, conflicts are red, treaties and other events amber, disasters teal and people blue. You can narrow it to an era, look from 10 to 500 km around a place, star entries to keep in My List, and copy a link that opens exactly what you're looking at.

Where the information comes from

Every battle, event and person comes from Wikidata, the free, openly licensed database that sits behind Wikipedia, and links to its Wikipedia article. Groundwar adds no events of its own and doesn't rewrite what the sources say. Its work is putting them on a map: choosing which kinds of events belong there, finding where each one happened, and measuring how far it is from the place you searched.

  • Locations come from each entry's coordinates on Wikidata. Where Wikidata records only the place an event took place in, with no exact spot, Groundwar pins it there, but only when that's a single, specific place (a town, a site or an island, not a whole region or a long river), and the entry says its location is approximate.
  • Dates are Wikidata's. Where only a year is known, that's all that's shown.
  • What counts as history: massacres and mass shootings appear only up to the end of the Second World War, so the map stays a map of history rather than of recent crime.
  • Article summaries in the app come from Wikipedia, the map from OpenStreetMap, and address search from Nominatim.

How it's kept up to date

Every Monday the whole map is rebuilt from Wikidata, so new articles, added locations and corrections made there reach Groundwar within a week, and each place page shows when it last changed. Messages sent through the contact form are read, and a missing location or a wrong date is fixed on Wikidata itself where possible, so the correction helps everyone who uses that data, not only this site.

What it can't tell you

  • It shows what's written up on Wikipedia and Wikidata, so well-documented places, wars and people appear far more than others. A place with nothing nearby may simply have had its history written in another language.
  • Distances are straight-line, as the crow flies, not travel distance.
  • Some dates and locations are approximate, or missing on Wikidata, and an event with no location there can't be pinned at all.

Who runs it

Groundwar is an independent project. It's free to use, needs no account, keeps nothing about you, and is supported by a single ad below the search results; the privacy policy explains what that involves. To report a problem, suggest something, or just say hello, use the contact page or email groundwar.app@gmail.com.
